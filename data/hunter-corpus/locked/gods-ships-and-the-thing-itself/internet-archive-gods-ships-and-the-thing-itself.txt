Gods, Ships, and the Thing Itself 


Reading the Iliad and Odyssey as Documents of Power 


Thomas J. Bryan 
February 2026 


Every ancient epic has two layers. The surface layer is the story the audience was 
meant to inhabit: gods intervening in human affairs, heroes of divine parentage earning 
glory through violence, fate working itself out across ten years and ten thousand deaths. 
The deeper layer is the thing itself — the material reality of power, wealth, extraction, 
and factional conflict that the surface story simultaneously describes and conceals. The 
lliad and the Odyssey are two of the oldest surviving documents from Western 
civilization, and they are accordingly two of the oldest surviving examples of the 
mechanism this series has been analyzing: elite consciousness encoded in narrative 
form, presented to a population that receives the surface but not the depth. 


This paper strips the divine layer and reads what remains. The argument is that once 
you remove the gods — once you understand that divine intervention is the standard 
ancient method for representing elite agency without making it legible to the common 
audience — both epics become remarkably precise accounts of the political dynamics 
they were composed to legitimate. The Iliad is a document about an intra-elite factional 
conflict within a coalition of allied kingdoms, in which a young king of exceptional 
military capacity comes of age and begins to grasp how the extraction system he is part 
of actually works. The Odyssey is a document about a succession crisis, managed 
through the strategic deployment of narrative legitimation, in which a faction secures a 
throne through coordinated violence and then explains that violence to the population as 
a returning hero's righteous homecoming. Both epics are, in the deepest sense, political 
documents. The gods are the machinery by which that politics disappears into heaven. 


Il. The Catalogue of Ships: Reading the Constitution 


Most readers experience the Catalogue of Ships in Book II of the lliad as an interruption 
— a 265-line list of leaders, kingdoms, and vessel counts that stops the narrative cold 
before it has properly begun. This is precisely the wrong way to read it. The Catalogue 
is not an interruption. It is the foundation document of everything that follows. It is the 
material constitution of the Greek coalition: a precise accounting of who commands how 
much military force, and therefore of where power actually sits in this alliance. 


The numbers are specific and they are not decorative. Agamemnon commands 100 
ships — the largest single contingent in the entire Greek force. No one else comes 
close. Diomedes commands 80 ships from Argos; the Great Ajax 12 from Salamis; 


Odysseus 12 from Ithaca. Achilles commands 50 ships of Myrmidons from Phthia in 
Thessaly. The Catalogue lists 29 contingents totaling 1,186 ships, with command and 
troop counts for each. It is, in the vocabulary of modern political analysis, a precise 
statement of the balance of power within the coalition: who has what, who owes what to 
whom, and who, accordingly, is king of kings. 


Agamemnon is king of kings not because Zeus ordained it, though the epic presents it 
that way. He is king of kings because he showed up with the most ships. Military 
capacity is the substrate — the foundational material reality on which every claim of 
authority in the coalition rests. The divine authorization (Agamemnon's scepter, his 
lineage from Zeus through Tantalus and Pelops and Atreus) is the legitimating narrative 
constructed on top of that substrate. The Catalogue shows us the substrate directly, in 
numbers, before the legitimating narrative reasserts itself. 


The Catalogue also encodes something more subtle: the coalition's internal tensions are 
written into its structure. This is not a unified army with a single chain of command. It is 
an alliance of quasi-independent kings who have committed contingents of varying size 
to a shared military enterprise under Agamemnon's nominal overall command. Each 
king retains authority over his own men. Each king has his own interests, his own 
calculation of what this war is worth to him, and his own assessment of whether 
Agamemnon's leadership is serving those interests. The Catalogue is a map of both the 
Coalition's power and its fault lines — and the lliad, understood correctly, is the story of 
what happens when the man with the second most capable force decides that the 
arrangement is not serving his interests. 


Scholars have long noted that the Catalogue appears to preserve genuinely old 
geographic and political information — information consistent with the Late Bronze Age 
political landscape of Greece rather than the Archaic period when Homer composed. 
Whether this represents actual historical memory or political construction is secondary 
to the structural point: whoever assembled this catalogue understood that the authority 
relationships in the coalition derived from the material conditions it recorded. The ships 
count. That is the point. Everything else — the divine lineages, the epithets, the golden 
scepters — is the story told about the ships. 


ll. Achilles: A Young King Coming of Age 


The lliad begins with a quarrel, and most readers experience it as a personal dispute — 
two strong men in conflict over a woman and the honor she represents. This is the 
surface reading. The deeper reading requires understanding what Briseis actually is 
within the political economy of the Greek coalition, and wnat Agamemnon's seizure of 
her therefore represents. 


In the spoils system of the Trojan War, captured women are not primarily sexual 
property (though they are that too) and not primarily personal prizes in the modern 
sporting sense. They are the material returns on military investment — the concrete 


form in which the wealth generated by the war's violence is distributed among the 
coalition's members. When the Greeks sack a city or take prisoners, the spoils are 
divided according to a hierarchy that places Agamemnon at the apex: he gets first 
choice of everything. The common soldiers get what remains after the kings have taken 
their shares. This is the extraction system of the coalition, made visible in each 
distribution of plunder. 


Briseis was awarded to Achilles as recognition of his military contribution — his aristeia, 
his status as the best fighter in the army. When Agamemnon takes her back, he is not 
simply insulting Achilles in a personal way. He is demonstrating that the rules of 
distribution do not actually bind him — that his power within the coalition is sufficient to 
override Achilles's legitimate claim, and that Achilles has no recourse. He is showing 
Achilles the thing beneath the honorable language of the spoils system: that 
Agamemnon extracts what he wants, and Achilles's military excellence serves 
Agamemnon's enrichment more than it serves Achilles's own interests. 


This is the moment of political education at the center of the Iliad, and it is worth 
understanding as such. Achilles is young — the lliad consistently presents him as 
younger than Agamemnon, Odysseus, Nestor, and most of the coalition's senior figures. 
He is a king of exceptional military capacity who has been operating within the 
Coalition's framework without fully examining the terms of that framework. The seizure of 
Briseis forces the examination. He asks, for the first time, the most dangerous question 
available: does this arrangement benefit me? And the answer, when he thinks it 
through, is clearly no. 


His response — withdrawal from battle — is the only leverage available to him within 
the system's existing rules. He cannot simply take Briseis back by force without 
triggering a civil war within the coalition that would destroy the entire enterprise. He 
cannot appeal to a neutral authority; Agamemnon is the nominal authority. What he can 
do is demonstrate, by withholding his military capacity, how dependent Agamemnon's 
war actually is on Achilles's willingness to fight it. The subsequent Greek catastrophe 
without Achilles — the losses, the near-disaster at the ships, the desperate embassy in 
Book IX — is the proof of his leverage. The entire Iliad from Book | onward is a political 
demonstration: Achilles showing Agamemnon, and the audience, what the coalition's 
true military dependency actually looks like when the man the coalition most depends 
on refuses to participate. 


The parallel your earlier notes draw with Henry VIII is precise. Henry VIII came to the 
English throne young, in a court full of older, more experienced men who had spent 
decades managing the Tudor state in ways that served the state's institutional interests 
— and their own. The young king was powerful in theory and managed in practice. The 
process of recognizing the management, and deciding to resist it, is the coming-of-age 
story that both Henry and Achilles enact. Both are men of exceptional capacity who 
have been functioning as instruments of a system that uses them more than it serves 
them, and who eventually realize it. 


The lliad's Achilles does not simply rage. He strategizes. He goes to his mother Thetis, 
who goes to Zeus, who arranges that the Greeks will suffer without Achilles until 


Agamemnon is forced to acknowledge the injustice. In the stripped reading, this is 
Achilles using the networks available to him — his own divine lineage, his relationships 
with the gods who favor him — to apply political pressure on the man who wronged him. 
The gods are the mechanism. The political pressure is the thing. 


His final transformation after Patroclus's death is the completion of the arc. The grief is 
real — Homer is not naive about human emotion. But what Achilles does with the grief 
is politically significant. He returns to the fight not simply to mourn or to honor Patroclus 
but to demonstrate something specific: that when Achilles fights, the war's outcome is 
no longer in doubt. His killing of Hector, and the three-day dragging of Hector's body 
behind his chariot in full view of the Trojan walls and the Greek army, is a performance 
for an audience. It says, with unmistakable clarity: this is what | can do. This is what the 
coalition has been missing. Agamemnon did not kill Hector. Agamemnon could not have 
killed Hector. Achilles killed Hector. The king of kings owes his war's decisive moment to 
the man he dishonored. The Iliad ends not with Agamemnon's triumph but with 
Achilles's — and the triumphant power is young, clear-eyed about the terms of the 
arrangement, and no longer manageable in the old way. 


lil. The Divine Layer: How Elite Agency Disappears into 
Heaven 


The gods of the Iliad intervene constantly: turning spears aside, lifting warriors out of 
dangerous situations, blinding armies with mist, whispering counsel, inspiring fear or 
courage. The standard reading treats these interventions as the epic's engagement with 
the divine — Homer's world in which the gods genuinely participate in human affairs. 
The structural reading asks a different question: what is the political function of 
attributing these events to divine agency rather than to human decision? 


The answer, once the question is posed, is not obscure. Divine agency conceals elite 
agency from the common audience while performing the same narrative work. When a 
noble dies in battle at what appears to be an impossible moment — when the spear that 
should have killed him somehow misses — the explanation that a goddess deflected it 
serves multiple functions simultaneously. It explains the event without requiring the 
audience to ask who made what decision and why. It reinforces the belief system's 
claim that the gods participate actively in human affairs and favor specific individuals. 
And it removes from public visibility any human calculation that might lie beneath the 
event. 


The Paris episode is the example your reading identified and it is the cleanest one in the 
text. Paris — prince of Troy, the man whose abduction of Helen is the war's stated 
cause — faces Menelaus in single combat in Book III. The duel should end the war: 
winner takes Helen, loser concedes. Menelaus is clearly winning. Paris is about to die. 
And then Aphrodite wraps Paris in mist and transports him to his bedroom, where Helen 
finds him essentially hiding. 


Strip the goddess. What remains? A prince who ran from a fight. The problem this 
creates is significant: Paris has asked thousands of men to die in a war that is 
ostensibly about his honor, and he is personally unwilling to fight the man whose wife he 
took. This is a lethal image for any elite trying to maintain the loyalty of the soldiers 
fighting on his behalf. The common soldier watching a prince flee his personal combat 
has every reason to ask the question Thersites will ask explicitly in the next book: why 
are we dying for men who won't fight? 


The goddess resolves the problem. Paris didn't run — he was taken by Aphrodite, who 
is his divine patron and who removed him from the field for reasons of her own. The 
prince is blameless; the goddess acted. The soldier who watched what happened can 
now have a different relationship to what he saw: not a coward prince avoiding 
consequence, but a man under divine protection so powerful that not even a legitimate 
combat can reach him. The narrative does not change what happened. It changes how 
what happened is understood. This is the legitimating function of divine intervention at 
its most precise: protecting elite actors from the political consequences of their visible 
failures, by attributing those failures to divine causes that ordinary humans cannot 
question or contest. 


The pattern runs through the entire epic. Odysseus persuades the army to return from 
the ships not through his own political skill but through Athena's instruction. Diomedes 
drives the Trojans back because Athena empowers him. Hector kills Patroclus with 
Apollo's assistance. At every decisive moment, the god is present. The human 
calculation — the specific decision made by a specific person in a specific situation, for 
reasons that might be examined — disappears behind divine will. The bards who 
performed these epics were serving aristocratic patrons, singing in aristocratic halls to 
audiences that included both the elite and the common soldiers and retainers who 
served them. The divine layer served the patrons. It kept what actually happens at the 
elite level of decision-making invisible to the level below. 


IV. Thersites: The Cost of Truth Without Authority 


The Thersites episode in Book II is the lliad's most explicit statement of the mechanism 
the whole poem implicitly performs. It is so explicit that it feels almost like a self-aware 
commentary — the poet showing his audience exactly what happens when someone 
says the true thing without the institutional authority to say it. 


The setup: the Greek army, after nine years of siege and the disaster of Achilles's 
withdrawal, rushes for the ships when Agamemnon tests their morale by pretending to 
propose abandonment of the war. Odysseus, holding Agamemnon's scepter, beats 
them back to order. Then Thersites speaks. Homer introduces him with a sustained 
physical description that is worth reading carefully: bandy-legged, lame, 
rounded-shouldered, warped-headed, scantily haired. He is described as the ugliest 
man in the Greek army. This dehumanization precedes his speech. The audience is 
primed, before he opens his mouth, to reject what he says. 


What he says is accurate. Every single claim he makes about the coalition's extraction 
system is factually correct and acknowledged elsewhere in the text. Agamemnon does 
get first choice of plunder — that is how the system works. The common soldiers do the 
fighting and the dying — that is also how the system works. Agamemnon did seize 
Achilles's prize by 'his own arrogant act' — even Nestor, one of Agamemnon's allies, 
says so in Book I. Thersites's conclusion — that the soldiers should leave Agamemnon 
here to ‘digest his prizes' and see how much the war goes for him without their 
participation — is the same conclusion Achilles has reached and is acting on. Thersites 
speaks objective truth about the extraction mechanism he and the common soldiers are 
embedded in. 


Odysseus's response is the demonstration of how authority works. He does not refute a 
single factual claim. He attacks Thersites personally — calls him the vilest mortal in the 
army, forbids him from speaking the names of kings, dismisses his argument as 
‘prattling and railings.’ He threatens to strip Thersites naked and beat him to the ships. 
And then he does beat him — with Agamemnon's golden scepter, the physical symbol 
of hierarchical authority — leaving a bloody weal across Thersites's back. The army 
laughs. 


The army laughs at a man who just told them the truth about their situation, immediately 
after the man with the symbol of authority beat him for it. This is not irrationality. It is the 
compliance mechanism working exactly as designed. The soldiers laugh because the 
social cost of not laughing — of being associated with the man the authority-holder just 
punished — is higher than the cognitive dissonance of laughing at an accurate critique 
of their own exploitation. The consciousness asymmetry between Thersites (who 
understands the extraction system and names it) and the common soldiers (who inhabit 
it but have been given no framework for understanding it as a system) is maintained not 
by force alone but by the social pressure of the crowd and the visible demonstration of 
what happens to those who speak without a scepter. 


The episode is remarkable because it encodes the mechanism so explicitly. The scepter 
does not validate truth. It overrides the need for truth validation. Odysseus is factually 
wrong and Thersites is factually right, and Odysseus wins completely because he holds 
the symbol. This is the Iliad commenting on itself: the bard who performs the story of the 
gods protecting elite actors from consequence is also performing, in the Thersites 
episode, an account of what happens to the person who notices that those protections 
are operating and says so. The story that legitimates the elite includes, embedded 
within it, the punishment of the person who tries to read it against the grain. The scepter 
beats him. The crowd laughs. And the poem goes on. 


V. The Odyssey: A Succession Crisis in Heroic Dress 


The Odyssey is a harder case than the Iliad because its political dynamics are more 
thoroughly concealed beneath its narrative surface. The lliad's factional conflict is visible 
— you can see Agamemnon and Achilles fighting, you can count the ships, you can 


hear Thersites being beaten. The Odyssey presents what appears to be a simpler story: 
faithful wife, wandering hero, patient son, triumphant homecoming. The stripping 
operation requires more work, but the political structure beneath is, once revealed, 
equally legible. 


Start with the premise the epic asks you to accept: a king has been absent for twenty 
years. Ten years of war, ten years of wandering. In the political reality of Late Bronze 
Age kingdoms — where kingship was personal authority, where there was no 
bureaucratic state to maintain order in a ruler's absence, where neighboring powers 
would probe any weakness and internal factions would consolidate around any power 
vacuum — a twenty-year absence is not survivable. Medieval kings lost their thrones 
after absences of five years. Richard | of England nearly lost his to his own brother after 
five years on crusade. The premise of the Odyssey is politically impossible, and the 
impossibility is significant, because it requires explanation. 


The explanation the epic provides — ten years of wandering through divine 
interference, Poseidon's wrath, Calypso's detention — is the standard divine-layer 
explanation. Strip the gods, and the remaining explanation is the one your reading 
proposes and which the political logic supports: Odysseus died during or shortly after 
the Trojan War. His kingdom experienced what Bronze Age kingdoms experiencing the 
death of their king always experienced: a succession crisis. The epic is the legitimating 
narrative constructed around that crisis — not a description of what happened, but the 
story that was told about what happened in order to resolve who had the right to rule 
afterward. 


The suitors, in this reading, are not primarily sexual predators with designs on 
Penelope. They are competing aristocratic factions from Ithaca and the neighboring 
islands, each seeking to establish a legitimate claim to governance of the kingdom 
through the one mechanism available: marriage to the queen of the house whose 
throne they want to occupy. The pattern is consistent across the historical record. Herod 
married Mariamne of the Hasmonean dynasty because conquest alone gave him no 
legitimate claim to Jewish kingship — the marriage converted military control into 
dynastic legitimacy. Henry VII married Elizabeth of York because his Tudor claim was 
thin and hers was Yorkist — the marriage absorbed the rival legitimacy. The suitors 
want to marry Penelope for the same reason: because Odysseus's son cannot be 
displaced by force alone without triggering a civil war, but the man who marries the 
widow of the dead king can claim the throne as a continuation of its lineage rather than 
as a usurpation. 


Penelope's famous stratagem — weaving Laertes's shroud by day, unraveling it by night 
— is not, in this reading, a love story. It is a regent's delaying tactic. The moment she 
selects a new husband, Telemachus's claim becomes contingent on that husband's 
goodwill. As long as she refuses to choose, Telemachus remains the legitimate heir with 
time to come of age and assert his claim independently. The weaving buys time. The 
suitors understand this, which is why they plot Telemachus's assassination when they 
learn he has gone to Pylos and Sparta — he is reaching the age of majority, at which 
point his claim strengthens dramatically and the suitors' window for forcing a marriage 
closes. 


The ‘return of Odysseus' narrative resolves the succession crisis elegantly. It provides a 
legitimate justification for the violent elimination of 108 aristocratic men that could not 
otherwise be achieved without triggering a multi-kingdom civil war. If Telemachus's 
faction simply assassinated the suitors, they would be conducting an obvious political 
purge — the kind of action that delegitimizes the faction conducting it and invites 
revenge from the victims' families. But if the rightful king has returned from his long 
wandering and is reclaiming his home from men who abused his household's hospitality 
— the laws of xenia, which were sacred in this world — then the massacre is not a 
purge. It is justice. It is a husband's righteous vengeance. It is the restoration of divine 
order after a long disruption. The population receives a story they can support. The 
political faction receives the throne. 


The rapid departure of Odysseus at the epic's end, hastily resolved in Book XXIV with a 
promise to go make peace with the suitors' families, supports this reading. An actual 
returned king who had genuinely been away for twenty years would not immediately 
leave again. He would rule. The post-Homeric tradition in the Telegony has Odysseus 
die abroad, killed by a son from Circe. The man who 'returned' does not stay to govern. 
Because an impostor cannot sustain the performance indefinitely, because the inner 
circle that authenticated the 'return' knows the truth, and because the political purpose 
of the return has been achieved — Telemachus's claim is established, the rival faction is 
eliminated, the narrative is in place — departure is the only available option. The heir 
rules. The ‘father’ leaves. The story holds. 


VI. The Wandering as Embedded Ideology 


The wandering tales that fill Books IX through XII of the Odyssey — the Cyclops, the 
Lotus Eaters, Circe, the Sirens, the Cattle of the Sun — deserve separate analysis 
because they perform a distinct ideological function from the succession narrative. They 
are not primarily plot. They are propaganda, embedded within the legitimating narrative, 
aimed at the specific populations the legitimating narrative needed to manage. 


The Cyclops episode is the most important and the most legible. Polyphemus is 
described as living without laws, without councils, without agriculture — he tends his 
sheep on a wild island and eats whatever is available, including, when they arrive, 
Odysseus's men. He is ‘lawless,’ ‘arrogant,’ beyond the reach of Zeus's authority. He is, 
in the stripped reading, a representation of the hunter-gatherer or pastoral mode of 
existence — the alternative to the agricultural civilization that the Mycenaean elite 
governs and extracts from. 


The political problem this alternative poses is not abstract. Agricultural surplus 
economies require people to stay in one place, cultivate land, produce surplus, and 
hand a portion of that surplus to the governing elite. The alternative — moving when 
resources are scarce, living outside the law's reach, answering to no authority — is 
always available to people who are sufficiently desperate or sufficiently calculating. Elite 
governing a settled agricultural population always faces the question of why the 


population stays. Physical coercion is expensive. Belief infrastructure does much of the 
work. But narrative that represents the alternative as monstrous — as the world of 
man-eating giants who live beyond civilization's protections — does some of the work 
too. The Cyclops teaches the audience: life outside the law is life among monsters who 
will eat you. Agricultural civilization, with its laws and its gods and its hierarchy and its 
extraction, is the price of safety. The alternative is Polyphemus. 


The Lotus Eaters represent the seduction of ease — the appeal of forgetting obligation, 
forgetting home, forgetting the duties that bind people to the agricultural and political 
order. The men who eat the lotus forget their purpose and have to be dragged back to 
the ships by force. The message is explicit: the desire to rest, to escape toil, to abandon 
duty, is a kind of death. The good man, the obedient man, the man who will succeed 
and return home, resists the lotus and keeps moving. The episode naturalizes the 
connection between obedience and survival, between duty and homecoming. 


The Cattle of the Sun is the explicit statement of the theme: Odysseus's men, 
specifically warned not to eat the sacred cattle of Helios, do so anyway when hunger 
becomes sufficient. All of them die. The epic presents this as divine punishment for 
sacrilege, which is the theological explanation. The political explanation is equivalent: 
disobedience of the established divine order, when motivated by mere physical need, 
results in death. The man who obeys — who maintains the sacred prohibitions even 
when hungry, even when comfort is available through violation — is the man who 
survives and achieves his goal. Odysseus survives because he obeys. His men die 
because they don't. The tale is a parable of compliance: want what you want, be hungry 
when you're hungry, but do not let your needs override the sacred obligations the belief 
system has established, because the gods will destroy you for it. 


These tales travel with the legitimating narrative of the 'return of Odysseus' and 
reinforce it ideologically. The audience that receives the succession story — that learns 
the righteous king came home, killed the transgressors, and restored divine order — 
also receives, embedded within it, a systematic account of what happens to people who 
violate hierarchy, abandon obligation, desire ease over duty, or put their hunger above 
sacred law. The epic package is complete: political legitimation of the ruling faction plus 
ideological preparation of the population to remain within the system that faction 
governs. 


Vil. Homer as Interpretive Class 


The final element of the analysis is the one the framework always asks: who produced 
the text, for whom, and what did they gain from it? 


Homer — or the tradition of bards in which 'Homer' is the name we give to the 
culminating synthesis — composed and performed for aristocratic patrons. Bards in the 
Greek tradition were not independent artists. They performed in the halls of powerful 
men, at the feasts of kings, for audiences that included the elite and those who served 


them. Their livelinood depended on patronage, and patronage flowed from the men 
whose stories the bards were telling. The tradition of oral epic preserved what the 
patrons wanted preserved and elaborated it in the ways that served the patrons’ 
interests. This is not a conspiracy. It is the normal operation of any 
patronage-dependent interpretive class. The bard who tells stories that embarrass his 
patrons does not eat. The bard who tells stories that glorify them, that legitimate their 
authority, that make their extraction appear divinely sanctioned and their enemies 
appear monstrous, finds steady employment. 


The lliad glorifies the Mycenaean warrior elite. It presents their quarrels as worthy of 
divine attention, their deaths as cosmically significant, their lineages as genuinely 
divine. It provides the common soldier who fights and dies in their wars with a 
framework in which that sacrifice has meaning: the gods are watching, glory is real, 
kleos (fame) persists after death. The man who dies at Troy is not merely a dead man in 
service of someone else's extraction project. He is a participant in a divinely ordered 
competition for glory, remembered in song, his death made meaningful by the narrative 
that preserves it. The bard provides this meaning. The bard is paid by the people whose 
wars are made meaningful. The arrangement works for everyone except Thersites, who 
understood that it was an arrangement and said so, and was beaten for it. 


The Odyssey serves a slightly different audience — the Archaic Greek aristocracies of 
the eighth century BCE, who were constructing their own political legitimacy in the 
aftermath of the Bronze Age collapse, using stories of Mycenaean heroes to establish 
their claims to continuity with a glorious past. The Odyssey teaches several things its 
aristocratic patrons needed their populations to know: that the laws of hospitality (xenia) 
are sacred and divinely enforced, that the violation of those laws justifies violence in 
response, that the patient, obedient, clever man succeeds while the impatient and the 
disobedient die, and that kings who have been absent return to reclaim what is rightfully 
theirs. All of these lessons serve elite interests. All of them are embedded in a narrative 
that presents them not as lessons but as the way things simply are — the divine order 
made visible through story. 


The bard who composed the Odyssey's final form was not necessarily a propagandist in 
any self-aware sense. He was a craftsman of narrative working within a tradition that 
had been shaped by centuries of aristocratic patronage to produce exactly the kind of 
stories aristocrats needed. The tradition did the ideological work. Individual bards within 
it were simply doing their craft well. This is how interpretive classes always operate 
most effectively: not through conscious manipulation but through the genuine 
internalization of a framework that happens to serve the interests of those who fund the 
framework's maintenance. Homer believed in the gods. Homer believed in kleos and the 
divine right of kings. Homer's belief made him a better bard for the elite he served, 
because sincerity in the legitimating narrative is always more effective than 
performance. The framework's principle — that sincerity is analytically irrelevant — 
applies here exactly: whether Homer believed it or not, the function is the same. 


Vill. What the Poems Know 


There is one final thing worth saying about the lliad and Odyssey that the stripped 
reading reveals, and it is something the poems know about themselves that the 
standard reading tends to obscure. 


The Iliad is not straightforwardly pro-Agamemnon. If it were simply elite propaganda, it 
would present Agamemnon as admirable and right, Achilles as wrong to withdraw, and 
the extraction system as just and natural. It does not do this. It presents Agamemnon as 
aman whose power rests on material conditions rather than personal excellence — as 
someone who is king of kings because of his ships, not his virtue. It acknowledges, 
through Nestor, through the embassy, through the entire structure of the poem, that 
Achilles was wronged. It gives Thersites accurate arguments. It shows the common 
soldiers ready to run for home after nine years. 


This complexity is not an accident. The most effective legitimating narratives are not 
simple ones — they are ones sophisticated enough to acknowledge real tensions while 
ultimately resolving them in ways that preserve the fundamental hierarchy. The lliad 
legitimates the Mycenaean elite not by pretending the elite is perfect but by showing 
that even when the elite is flawed — even when Agamemnon is greedy and Achilles is 
right — the divine framework within which they operate is real, the glory they pursue is 
real, and the alternative (running back to the ships, the world of Thersites, the chaos of 
a Coalition without its king of kings) is worse. The poem validates your grievance and 
then gives you a reason to fight anyway. This is sophisticated ideology. 


The Odyssey is more schematic — the patient obedient man wins, the disobedient die, 
the lawless Cyclops gets blinded, the suitors get massacred — but even here there are 
traces of the thing the poem is managing. Penelope's intelligence is as obvious as 
Odysseus's. Telemachus's political education in Books | through IV, in which he learns 
to assert himself and build the coalitions he needs, is a recognizable coming-of-age 
story that the audience can read as their own. The poem knows that the return narrative 
is thin — that twenty years is too long, that the recognitions are too convenient — and 
compensates with the emotional weight of Penelope's faithfulness, Telemachus's 
growth, the old dog Argos dying of joy at his master's return. The emotional power does 
the work that the narrative logic cannot quite sustain. 


Both poems are, in the deepest sense, about the consciousness asymmetry. The lliad 
dramatizes a moment when a member of the conscious elite — Achilles — sees the 
extraction system from outside and refuses it. The Thersites episode dramatizes a 
moment when a member of the common population sees it and is beaten back in. The 
Odyssey dramatizes the reconstruction of legitimacy after a crisis, the production of a 
narrative powerful enough to make a political purge feel like a homecoming. These are 
the operations of the framework made visible in verse. They are remarkable not 
because they reveal cynicism — the poets and their patrons were not cynics — but 
because they preserve, in the formal constraints of the hexameter, the exact dynamics 
that the framework has been analyzing across civilizations and millennia. 


The gods are the machinery. The thing itself — the ships, the spoils, the succession 
crisis, the scepter that beats the truth-teller — is underneath. It has always been 
underneath. Reading Homer against the grain is reading him accurately. 


A Note on Method and This Series 


This paper applies the analytical framework developed across the Patterns of Power 
series to Homer's Iliad and Odyssey. The core methodological move — stripping the 
divine layer and reading the material political dynamics beneath — is the 'Reading 
Against the Grain’ technique developed in the series’ analytical framework for 
pre-modern sources. Ancient texts were produced by and for elites; their divine 
narratives are not neutral descriptions of events but products of the same legitimating 
mechanisms the framework analyzes. Reading them as such reveals dynamics that the 
standard literary readings, focused on the surface narrative, cannot access. 


The series has now covered Egyptian Ma’at, the Abrahamic tradition across three 
founding figures, Islamic divine authorization, the Chinese Mandate, Western divine 
right and constitutional legitimacy, inter-civilizational dynamics, and elite factional 
conflict in Western civilization. Homer was always the intended starting point — the 
oldest surviving Western texts, written by a member of the interpretive class for 
aristocratic patrons, about a world where divine intervention is the standard mechanism 
for concealing elite agency. Reading him first would have been intuitive. Reading him 
last reveals how precisely his texts fit the framework that two years of case studies built 
to describe them.
